​​​ ​This brick building here is the​ ​​ ​​ ​Jamestown museum in Virginia,​ ​​
​​​ ​home to what's known as the first​ ​​ ​​ ​successful English colony in America.​ ​​
​​​ ​But it wasn't easy getting there.​ ​​
​​​ ​Disease,​ ​​
​​​ ​starvation, and...​ ​​
​​​ ​even cannibalism nearly wiped out the colony.​ ​​
​​​ ​Yet here we are centuries later,​ ​​ ​​ ​walking through the same place​ ​​
​​​ ​by paying around 20 bucks to get in! Whoopee!​ ​​
​​​ ​Get ready for some history info dump.​ ​​
​​​ ​Jamestown was the first permanent​ ​​ ​​ ​English settlement in America,​ ​​
​​​ ​established by the Virginia Company.​ ​​
​​​ ​They chose this spot because​ ​​ ​​ ​it was easy to defend,​ ​​
​​​ ​and it's right next to the James River,​ ​​ ​​ ​so they could spot ships coming in.​ ​​
​​​ ​Unfortunately, the terrain was​ ​​ ​​ ​plagued by swampy land,​ ​​
​​​ ​mosquito-born disease (buzz buzz),​ ​​
​​​ ​scarce food, and undrinkable water,​ ​​
​​​ ​making survival brutal.​ ​​
​​​ ​Now, if you ever decide to visit Jamestown,​ ​​
​​​ ​the main appeal is being able to walk around​ ​​
​​​ ​and take in the sights at whatever​ ​​ ​​ ​pace you want for yourself.​ ​​
​​​ ​So you're going to see things that resemble​ ​​ ​​ ​the structures from way back then.​ ​​
​​​ ​Depending on when you visit,​ ​​
​​​ ​you might be lucky enough to meet​ ​​ ​​ ​the historians dressed in period clothing,​ ​​
​​​ ​and they'll share with you what they​ ​​ ​​ ​know about the village's history.​ ​​
​​​ ​Inside, things were kept pretty simple.​ ​​
​​​ ​You got wooden tables, cookware,​ ​​ ​​ ​armor, equipment...​ ​​
​​​ ​And very little privacy.​ ​​
​​​ ​Such a small space that they had to be​ ​​ ​​ ​very particular with what they keep.​ ​​
​​​ ​It would be so over for me, guys.​ ​​
​​​ ​I have way too much merch and​ ​​ ​​ ​way too many clothes to live there.​ ​​
​​​ ​Some of the houses did have a second floor,​ ​​
​​​ ​but it was barred off,​ ​​ ​​ ​so no idea what's up there.​ ​​
​​​ ​Who knows, maybe they have colonial merch.​ ​​
​​​ ​This house, for example,​ ​​ ​​ ​I'm pretty sure belonged to the more rich folks.​ ​​
​​​ ​Maybe you've heard of Captain John Smith,​ ​​
​​​ ​because he led the small colony in its early years.​ ​​
​​​ ​During his leadership, there was this rough​ ​​ ​​ ​period known as the "Starving Time."​ ​​
​​​ ​Out of the 500 original settlers,​ ​​ ​​ ​only 60 were left alive.​ ​​
​​​ ​Basically supply ships either sank​ ​​ ​​ ​or they just arrived way too late,​ ​​
​​​ ​so the colony had no food and was starving.​ ​​
​​​ ​Initially, the Powhatan people traded with settlers.​ ​​
​​​ ​But when Jamestown kept growing and growing,​ ​​
​​​ ​the tribe just couldn't keep up​ ​​ ​​ ​with their increasing demands.​ ​​
​​​ ​When trade wasn't enough,​ ​​
​​​ ​the settlers resorted to raiding Powhatan​ ​​ ​​ ​villages for food and supplies,​ ​​
​​​ ​even burning some of the houses​ ​​ ​​ ​and kidnapping Pocahontas—​ ​​
​​​ ​the daughter of Chief Powhatan—​ ​​
​​​ ​to use as leverage during negotiations.​ ​​
​​​ ​To make things worse,​ ​​
​​​ ​the Powhatan people were killing​ ​​ ​​ ​anyone who tried to leave the fort,​ ​​
​​​ ​making hunting and farming impossible.​ ​​
​​​ ​Settlers ended up eating horses, dogs, rats...​ ​​
​​​ ​Even the leather from their shoes.​ ​​
​​​ ​And some accounts even mention cannibalism.​ ​​
​​​ ​Meanwhile, the Powhatan people already had​ ​​ ​​ ​homes built to handle the environment.​ ​​
​​​ ​Their yehakins were made from​ ​​ ​​ ​woven mats and wooden frames,​ ​​
​​​ ​and you can see some of these​ ​​ ​​ ​on the venue if you choose to visit.​ ​​
​​​ ​This is a native American cabin.​ ​​
​​​ ​Inside, it's simple.​ ​​
​​​ ​Pelts spread across beds,​ ​​
​​​ ​maybe even hanging on the walls​ ​​ ​​ ​or laid on the floors to insulate the home.​ ​​
​​​ ​Above the beds is what looks like a drying rack,​ ​​
​​​ ​used to hang up hides, furs, or clothes.​ ​​
​​​ ​This tight bundle of sticks may be firewood​ ​​ ​​ ​storage or used for some other material.​ ​​
​​​ ​The pelts looked so fuzzy and cozy.​ ​​
​​​ ​I wanted to touch it,​ ​​ ​​ ​but I also thought it was probably unclean.​ ​​
​​​ ​That didn't stop Plushvella, though.​ ​​
​​​ ​Despite the early mortality​ ​​ ​​ ​rates and the hardships,​ ​​
​​​ ​Jamestown is considered a success,​ ​​
​​​ ​especially once John Rolfe​ ​​ ​​ ​introduced tobacco to the economy.​ ​​
​​​ ​With that, let's move on to Yorktown.​ ​​
​​​ ​My museum ticket came bundled with Yorktown,​ ​​
​​​ ​and with the two exhibits close to each other,​ ​​ ​​ ​I figured why not visit.​ ​​
​​​ ​When I first entered,​ ​​ ​​ ​to my side were marching silhouttes of soldiers,​ ​​
​​​ ​preparing for battle against the British.​ ​​
​​​ ​Yorktown is important, because this was​ ​​ ​​ ​about America's fight for independence.​ ​​
​​​ ​And the Battle of Great Bridge is a turning​ ​​ ​​ ​point in history as America's first victory.​ ​​
​​​ ​The bridge itself was a key route​ ​​ ​​ ​that connected two sides of the river,​ ​​
​​​ ​so it was important to access it​ ​​ ​​ ​to control the region.​ ​​
​​​ ​Behold! More words.​ ​​
​​​ ​This is Thomas Jefferson.​ ​​
​​​ ​He's on the nickels and drafted the​ ​​ ​​ ​Declaration of Independence,​ ​​
​​​ ​which separated America from Britain.​ ​​
​​​ ​Over there, you got Big Ben—​ ​​ ​​ ​famous dirty kite flyer.​ ​​
​​​ ​But nah, aside from electricity,​ ​​ ​​ ​he secured the French alliance for Americans.​ ​​
​​​ ​For those who don't know, France and Britain​ ​​ ​​ ​have a history of hating each other.​ ​​
​​​ ​There are also notes on the Home Front,​ ​​
​​​ ​which is about how communities​ ​​ ​​ ​managed and supported the war effort.​ ​​
​​​ ​Spy networks,​ ​​
​​​ ​writing,​ ​​
​​​ ​WOMEN,​ ​​
​​​ ​and neighborhood efforts​ ​​ ​​ ​like farming, sewing, and so on.​ ​​
​​​ ​There was this personality quiz on this giant​ ​​ ​​ ​display at some point in the museum.​ ​​
​​​ ​And it was pretty cool, honestly.​ ​​
​​​ ​It asks you maybe five questions​ ​​ ​​ ​on how you'd handle these certain situations.​ ​​
​​​ ​And then it matches your answers​ ​​
​​​ ​based on other historical figures​ ​​ ​​ ​who did do something similar.​ ​​
​​​ ​This is one of my results,​ ​​
​​​ ​and this person for example​ ​​ ​​ ​was a writer, playwright,​ ​​
​​​ ​and a strong critic against British rule.​ ​​
​​​ ​Her writing stirred revolutionary​ ​​ ​​ ​sentiment in the colony,​ ​​
​​​ ​and she wrote a history​ ​​ ​​ ​of the American Revolution,​ ​​
​​​ ​making her one of the first women​ ​​ ​​ ​to provide detailed accounts of war.​ ​​
​​​ ​The museum was packed with a lot information.​ ​​
​​​ ​Plaques about generals,​ ​​
​​​ ​stories from soldiers,​ ​​
​​​ ​tons of historical maps,​ ​​
​​​ ​and even a holographic display​ ​​
​​​ ​where actors reenacted what it was like​ ​​ ​​ ​to be a soldier back then.​ ​​
​​​ ​There's also a mini theater that plays​ ​​ ​​ ​short historical films at set intervals.​ ​​

​​​ ​Outside, they had full recreations​ ​​ ​​ ​of military encampments,​ ​​
​​​ ​and I was lucky enough to catch​ ​​ ​​ ​some re-enactors in action.​ ​​

​​​ ​Basically, soldiers primarily​ ​​ ​​ ​ate bean and meat stew.​ ​​
​​​ ​In that left bowl is a hardtack with split peas.​ ​​
​​​ ​The dry biscuit was meant to last for​ ​​ ​​ ​a long time without spoiling,​ ​​
​​​ ​and they would soak it into soup to make it edible.​ ​​
​​​ ​If it's too dry, it can actually break your teeth.​ ​​
​​​ ​There was also a medic (or surgeon)​ ​​
​​​ ​who demonstrated the kinds of​ ​​ ​​ ​tools they used back then,​ ​​
​​​ ​which was both cool and a little terrifying.​ ​​
​​​ ​On the table you have a few tools.​ ​​
​​​ ​You have the bone saw,​ ​​ ​​ ​used for amputations without any anesthesia.​ ​​
​​​ ​The cautery iron would seal​ ​​ ​​ ​the wound with burning hot iron.​ ​​
​​​ ​Bullet extractors.​ ​​ ​​ ​They'd remove any shrapnel or musket balls,​ ​​
​​​ ​otherwise you might have an infection.​ ​​
​​​ ​The tooth key.​ ​​
​​​ ​If a tooth was decayed or infected,​ ​​ ​​ ​they would wrench it out.​ ​​
​​​ ​They might even accidentally​ ​​ ​​ ​break the tooth or jaw during this.​ ​​
​​​ ​And you got a toothbrush.​ ​​ ​​ ​Made out of twigs.​ ​​
​​​ ​Oh, and in the back there were some​ ​​ ​​ ​farmhouses you could explore.​ ​​
​​​ ​And a cute little cat​ ​​ ​​ ​that they told you not to pet.​ ​​
​​​ ​Not sure who the cat belongs to,​ ​​ ​​ ​but don't touch it and don't touch the chickens.​ ​​

​​​ ​They even had a small seasonal farm in the back,​ ​​
​​​ ​plus walking, live rotisserie.​ ​​

​​​ ​So if you like history​ ​​ ​​ ​or seeing things from the past,​ ​​
​​​ ​maybe go the museum,​ ​​ ​​ ​'cause I had a lot of fun there.​ ​​
​​​ ​Overall, really fun trip at the museum.​ ​​
​​​ ​Yorktown being my favorite between the two.​ ​​

​​​ ​If you're not into museums,​ ​​ ​​ ​what about a little ghost hunting instead?​ ​​
​​​ ​At night, Williamsburg takes on​ ​​ ​​ ​an entirely different tone.​ ​​
​​​ ​Flickering torchlight,​ ​​
​​​ ​boots echoing on old brick,​ ​​
​​​ ​and guides dressed in colonial wear​ ​​
​​​ ​as they lead you through one of the​ ​​ ​​ ​most haunted towns in America.​ ​​
​​​ ​For this ghost tour,​ ​​ ​​ ​we were split into three groups​ ​​
​​​ ​and led through three different locations.​ ​​
​​​ ​An old courthouse​ ​​ ​​ ​and two preserved colonial homes.​ ​​
​​​ ​For the courthouse,​ ​​ ​​ ​the guide told us about the trials held there.​ ​​
​​​ ​Executions, public punishments...​ ​​
​​​ ​Typical stuff from the era.​ ​​
​​​ ​But one story stood out.​ ​​
​​​ ​It was about a homeless man​ ​​ ​​ ​with an enormous debt.​ ​​
​​​ ​There wasn't a proper "jail"​ ​​ ​​ ​in the town at the time,​ ​​
​​​ ​so when the villagers got tired of him,​ ​​
​​​ ​they locked him up in a makeshift prison​ ​​
​​​ ​while they figured out what to do with him.​ ​​
​​​ ​On one night, when the sheriff's daughter​ ​​ ​​ ​came by to bring him food,​ ​​
​​​ ​she heard voices from his cell.​ ​​
​​​ ​It sounded like he was​ ​​ ​​ ​having a full conversation,​ ​​
​​​ ​but the voice that he spoke to​ ​​ ​​ ​was deeper, slower...​ ​​
​​​ ​Unnatural.​ ​​
​​​ ​After opening the little sliding slot to peek in,​ ​​
​​​ ​she found him standing there,​ ​​
​​​ ​staring right at the wall,​ ​​ ​​ ​talking to the "devil."​ ​​
​​​ ​The accounts after that are muddied.​ ​​
​​​ ​No one knows exactly​ ​​ ​​ ​what happened to him after.​ ​​
​​​ ​Some stories say he went blind,​ ​​ ​​ ​some say he died.​ ​​
​​​ ​As for the girl,​ ​​ ​​ ​she was never the same again.​ ​​
​​​ ​After that, we visited the​ ​​ ​​ ​Peyton Randolph House,​ ​​
​​​ ​which is considered as the most​ ​​ ​​ ​haunted home in all of Williamsburg.​ ​​
​​​ ​It was more of a collection of spirits​ ​​ ​​ ​instead of just one restless entity.​ ​​
​​​ ​Stories of children who died young,​ ​​
​​​ ​soldiers who never came home,​ ​​
​​​ ​and silhouettes of people​ ​​ ​​ ​dressed from a different time.​ ​​
​​​ ​The last house was smaller,​ ​​
​​​ ​and tucked away in the middle​ ​​ ​​ ​of town near a church.​ ​​
​​​ ​Like the other house,​ ​​ ​​ ​there was no electricity,​ ​​
​​​ ​only oil lamps to light up the way.​ ​​
​​​ ​There was a checkerboard floor​ ​​ ​​ ​in the room we sat in,​ ​​
​​​ ​and the guide shared simpler​ ​​ ​​ ​stories to end the night with.​ ​​
​​​ ​Nothing dramatic.​ ​​ ​​ ​Just a cozy end to tie up the tour.​ ​​
